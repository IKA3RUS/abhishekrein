<Section id="context">

In early 2026, [**amazon** announced the expansion of **amazon
now**](https://www.aboutamazon.in/news/retail/amazon-now-india-ultra-fast-delivery)
\- its quick commerce platform - to 100 cities across India as part of a ₹2,800
crore investment in its operations network. The plan called for over 1,000
darkstores.

In cities where those couldn't be stood up fast enough, **more**'s 800
supermarkets were the ready infrastructure. **amazon** holds a 49% stake in
**more** and **more**'s supermarkets had been the backbone of **amazon fresh**'s
time-slotted deliveries for years. This relationship had real operational
history, but **amazon now** posed a new question: whether the same stores could
operate at hyperlocal quick-commerce speed.

**more** had to retrofit traditional supermarkets for sub-10-minute deliveries.
That meant that every **more** supermarket had to: pick orders fast, keep
inventory honest, and give managers complete visibility over and control of the
floor. This study explores how we built the systems that let **amazon** and
**more** execute that ambition.

## zero cognitive load <br /> at the shelf

A picker navigating a live supermarket aisle has no attention to spare. The
picker app was built around that constraint.

Three primary states carry a picker through an order: waiting to accept, picking
each item in sequence, and packing into bags for the rack. Quick commerce runs
on the clock. The timer in every state gives pickers that same awareness.

## the ground realities <br /> of real supermarkets

A darkstore is a controlled environment. A supermarket is a public one, and
public environments are messy. Customers moves a product to the wrong shelves.
Sales happen while POS systems are offline. Barcodes gets scuffed by trolleys

These realities of the floor are why the picker app was designed with escape
hatches that let pickers resolve these situations without stalling.

## the only competition <br /> that matters

Speed is a skill. Picking orders fast is a skill. Like all skills, it improves
with feedback.

Pickers receive detailed performance rankings after every order -
but only against themselves. The leaderboard is personal by design. There's no
intentional race as a hostile floor helps no one.

## pathfinding & <br /> shelf expression

Supermarkets are busy places. Navigating crowded aisles with split-second
precision is genuinely difficult. High attrition in the sector means even
muscle memory can't be taken for granted.

I designed a signage system that gave pickers a reliable spatial grammar to
work with. The system was designed to work for both pickers racing through
aisles, and customers browsing at their own pace - in the same aisles.

## the (figurative) <br /> operating system

Store managers and duty managers are pulled in every direction during quick
commerce shifts - pickers raising issues, orders running late, the floor in
constant motion.

I designed **more os** as the (figurative) operating system for the supermarket,
giving store managers and duty managers a live picture of every active order,
every picker, and every open issue - from a single surface.

## the doubtful accuracy <br /> of planograms

Every system described above depends on one thing: knowing where a product
actually is. Planograms give us that, when they're accurate.

The same ground realities that slow pickers down also quietly corrupt
planograms over time. A product gets shelved at the wrong location. The system
never finds out.

We built an inventory application that closes this loop at the earliest possible
moment: when stock arrives at the storefront. Store staff scan and stow products
as they land, using the same shelf signages pickers rely on. Staff scan and stow
as products land, and any override updates the planogram automatically.

# reflections

Quick commerce, at least in this incarnation, is a logistics
problem dressed as a product problem. Most of our work here was invisible by
design: the picker app's job was to disappear into the motion of the job, the
signage to become spatial intuition, the planogram loop to run quietly in the
background.

> Every system is perfectly designed to get the results it gets.
> --- W. Edwards Deming

The system's resilience depended on people doing the right thing under time
pressure. That's the part no interface fully solves. Everything in this project
was an attempt to layer speed onto an environment designed for the opposite,
without breaking what made the environment work in the first place. The apps,
the signage, the tooling - coordination infrastructure. The 10 minutes customers
experience is the visible surface of a much larger system, most of which has
nothing to do with a screen.
